\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern Earth-observing (EO) satellites still operate with limited autonomy: ground-optimized schedules are uplinked and executed with little response to uncertainty.
This mode of operation works effectively when target utility is known or can be accurately forecast, but can waste opportunities when transient events such as wildfires, floods, or cloud cover change the utility of targets.
Cloud cover is the most common example: clouds obscure approximately 66\% of Earth's surface at any one time~\citep{king_spatial_2013}, obscuring a target when alternatives may be clear.

Dynamic tasking addresses this limitation by repairing the onboard schedule as new utility information becomes available.
That information may come from a secondary sensor, another spacecraft, or an external meteorological product.
We consider a secondary body-fixed, wide-field instrument paired with an onboard classifier that updates access utilities and a scheduler that repairs the affected schedule fragment (\figref{fig:conops}).

Acquiring new information has its own cost. A lookahead may reveal viable alternative images, but it consumes maneuver and inference time and may displace imaging already in the schedule.
Body-fixed lookahead is therefore a value-of-information problem: the spacecraft is not simply choosing its next image, but deciding whether an information-gathering maneuver will improve the downstream schedule enough to justify its cost.

Previous work searches directly over lookahead actions or fixes the observation strategy~\citep{kangaslahti_dynamic_2024, candela_dynamic_2022}; we instead optimize lookahead pitch and observe only when the expected repair value is positive.
Conventional execution and omniscient scheduling provide lower and upper reference policies, and their expected gap is the expected value of perfect information (EVPI)~\citep{howard_information_1966, raiffa_applied_1968}.
We report the empirical fraction of the realized gap closed by each dynamic-tasking policy as an EVSI-like performance measure, but do not identify it with optimal expected value of sample information because the evaluated policies are not themselves proven optimal.

Viewed through this lens, the systems analysis defines which information-gathering actions are physically available, the value model asks whether an action can repay its local schedule burden, and the timing and pointing rules maximize that margin within a schedule gap.

We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \textbf{systems analysis} linking ConOps, body-fixed field of view, geometry, and agility to feasible information-gathering actions (\secref{sec:systems_analysis}).
    \item A \textbf{two-anchor renewal heuristic} and monotone break-even gate for lookahead actions (\secref{sec:heuristic}).
    \item A \textbf{receding-horizon scheduler} combining pitch search, closed-form timing, and local repair (\secref{sec:lookahead_policy}--\secref{sec:schedule_repair}).
\end{enumerate}
